How to value a company, in plain English

They are a starting point, not a replacement for his work: the links in each section go to the originals.

The big idea: a story, then numbers

Every valuation is a story about a business turned into numbers. The story says how big the company can get, how profitable it can be and how much it has to invest to get there. The numbers then give a value you can compare with the market price.

Damodaran separates two different questions, and so does Fulcrum:

  • Value asks what the business is worth given its cash flows, growth and risk. This is the Valuation page.
  • Price asks what the market is paying relative to similar companies. This is the Peers page.

They can disagree, and that is useful. A relative-valuation view can be right while the whole group is overpriced, and the reverse is true too.

On this site: the sliders on the Valuation page are your story. The charts show what that story is worth, and what story the current price implies.

Intrinsic value: the DCF, step by step

A discounted cash flow adds up the cash a business will hand to its investors in the future, each year's amount shrunk back to today because a dollar later is worth less than a dollar now. Damodaran builds the cash flows from a few drivers rather than guessing the cash itself:

Revenue growthHow fast sales grow, fading toward the economy's pace.
Operating marginHow much of each sale is profit, moving to a target.
TaxStarts at what the firm pays and drifts to the normal rate.
ReinvestmentGrowth costs money: new capital, new factories, working capital.
Cost of capitalThe return investors demand; it falls toward a mature level.
Terminal valueEverything after the forecast, with strict limits.
Free cash flow to the firm = operating income × (1 − tax rate) − reinvestment

Reinvestment through sales-to-capital

Instead of forecasting capital spending line by line, this approach asks how much revenue each unit of new capital produces. If a company has to invest 1 of capital for every 2 of extra sales, its sales-to-capital ratio is 2. Divide the revenue you expect to add each year by that ratio and you have the reinvestment bill. High growth with a low ratio eats most of the profit, and that is the whole trade-off between growth and cash.

On this site: “Growth efficiency” is the sales-to-capital ratio. We start it from the company's own history (revenue divided by debt plus equity minus cash). The stacked chart shows each year's after-tax operating profit split into cash to investors and reinvestment.

Cost of capital

The discount rate blends what shareholders expect (a risk-free rate plus the company's beta times an equity risk premium) with the cost of borrowing, weighted by how the firm is financed. Damodaran publishes implied equity risk premiums and country risk premiums every January; Fulcrum's defaults use the January 2026 figures (about 4.5% for the US, about 7.1% for India). Betas from a single stock's price history are noisy, so Fulcrum nudges Yahoo's number toward 1, a common adjustment.

For a young or fast-changing company the discount rate should not stay put for ten years. In his model it holds for the early years and then converges to the rate of a mature company.

On this site: the cost of capital stays at your chosen level for years 1 to 5, then moves in a straight line to a mature-company rate by year 10. Open Advanced to change the risk-free rate, risk premium, beta or debt weight.

The terminal value rules

Most of a company's value usually sits after the forecast, so Damodaran is firm about how that last piece is built. Paraphrasing his lecture notes and his terminal-value paper:

1. Growth cannot beat the economy

A company that grows faster than the economy forever eventually becomes the economy. Perpetual growth has to stay below the discount rate, and he suggests using the risk-free rate as a practical ceiling because it already contains inflation and currency effects. Negative stable growth is allowed when it is justified.

2. Growth must be paid for

Growth equals the reinvestment rate times the return on invested capital, so the reinvestment rate in perpetuity is growth divided by return on capital. Setting reinvestment to zero in the terminal phase is a classic error: capital stops growing while profit keeps growing, and the implied return drifts toward infinity.

Reinvestment rate = g ÷ ROIC
Terminal value = Operating income(n+1) × (1 − t) × (1 − g ÷ ROIC) ÷ (cost of capital − g)

3. Excess returns are a choice, not a given

What drives terminal value is less the growth rate than the return earned on new investment. If competition erodes advantages, the return on capital falls to the cost of capital and growth adds nothing. If a company has a durable edge (a brand, a network, a patent, a cost advantage), returns can stay above the cost of capital for longer. He accepts that excess returns can outlast the high-growth phase, but wants you to say so explicitly.

On this site: the “Competitive advantage” switch sets the terminal return on capital: none means it equals the cost of capital, moderate adds 3 points, strong adds 6. Terminal growth is capped at the risk-free rate, and the reinvestment rate is computed from the formula above.

4. Be careful with exit multiples

Taking a multiple from today's peers and applying it at the end of a forecast quietly turns an intrinsic valuation into a pricing exercise; he calls these “Trojan Horse” DCFs. If you want a multiple at the end, derive it from your own growth, risk and returns assumptions. Fulcrum therefore does not use an exit multiple in the DCF.

5. A big terminal share is normal

Seeing 70 to 80 per cent or more of value from the terminal period is not a flaw by itself: investors in going concerns earn most of their return from price appreciation. The sensible response is to stress-test the assumptions that drive it.

Relative value: reading the Peers page

Relative valuation compares what the market pays for a company with what it pays for similar ones, using a multiple such as price to earnings. Damodaran's four steps, in short:

StandardiseTurn prices into multiples so different-sized firms can be compared.
Define consistentlyCalculate each multiple the same way for every firm.
Look at the spreadMultiples are skewed; check the middle, the outliers and the missing.
Control for differencesGrowth, risk and returns explain why multiples differ.

Match the numerator and the denominator

Equity-value multiples (price to earnings, price to book) divide by something that belongs to shareholders. Enterprise-value multiples (EV to EBITDA, EV to EBIT, EV to sales) divide by something that belongs to everyone who funded the firm, so debt must be added and cash taken away in the numerator. When you turn a multiple back into a price, do the same on the way out: an enterprise multiple gives firm value, and you subtract net debt before dividing by shares.

Use the median and mind the gaps

Multiples have a long right tail, so averages mislead; the median is usually the better guide. Throwing out outliers can bias a result if they are all on one side, and ignoring firms with negative earnings can too. Fulcrum leaves out multiples that are not defined (negative or absurdly large) and says how many companies each range is built from.

Cheap or dear for a reason

A low multiple can be fully deserved. Price to earnings rises with growth and payout and falls with risk; sales multiples depend on the margins you expect in future. Comparing multiples without controlling for these is the commonest way to fool yourself. One approach he shows is to regress a multiple on a fundamental such as growth and see where the company sits against the line.

He also suggests picking the one multiple that fits how the business creates value rather than averaging several. His rules of thumb: price to book for financial firms, EV to EBITDA for capital-heavy businesses, price to earnings for cyclical manufacturers on normalised earnings, sales multiples for young high-growth firms without profits or for retailers.

On this site: the Peers page shows medians and the middle half of peers (Q1 to Q3) for five multiples, converts each into an implied share price, and plots a growth-versus-multiple scatter with a regression line when there are enough companies. It also names the multiple that usually fits the company's sector.

How each kind of company is modelled

One recipe does not fit every business. Damodaran keeps the same logic (cash flows, reinvestment, risk, a terminal value that obeys the economy) but sets the inputs by the kind of business: a bank on equity and excess returns, a cyclical on its through-the-cycle margin, a young software firm on a margin it grows into. Fulcrum reads each company's industry from Yahoo Finance and picks one of the setups below. The starting points are Fulcrum's own, written in the spirit of his industry tables (not copies of them), and every one of them can be changed with the sliders.

Banks, lenders, insurersEquity model (excess returns)Valued on book equity and return on equity. No enterprise multiples.
SoftwareCash-flow DCFMargin given 8 years to mature, growth fades, stock pay counted as a real cost, higher risk.
SemiconductorsCash-flow DCFHalf the margin is the multi-year average, capital-hungry growth, high beta.
Hardware and electronicsCash-flow DCFMargin normalised and pulled toward peers, modest long-run growth.
Energy, materials, autos, transportCash-flow DCFMargin set on the average over the cycle, heavy reinvestment.
Utilities, telecomCash-flow DCFGrowth is expensive to fund (low sales-to-capital), low risk.
Internet, IT services, payments, pharma, retail, staples, industrialsCash-flow DCFEach has its own typical margin, capital efficiency and risk as a starting point.

What every cash-flow model does the same way

  • Growth is disciplined. Year 1 blends analyst consensus (when Yahoo has it) with the company's own history. Years 2 to 5 are pulled 30% back toward a normal long-run rate for that kind of business, because fast growth rarely lasts, and are capped by size: a company already earning hundreds of billions cannot double again and again.
  • Margins drift toward the industry. The target margin starts from the company's own and is pulled toward the peer median (or a typical level for its kind of business), less so when its returns on capital are far above its cost of capital.
  • Reinvestment is not taken at face value. Sales-to-capital blends the company's own book figure with a typical one for its industry, because buybacks shrink book capital and make a firm look more efficient than it is.
  • Risk is bottom-up. Beta is half the company's own and half a typical figure for its industry re-levered at its debt, because a single stock's price history is noisy. Cost of debt comes from interest cover, the way a credit rating is built.
  • The bridge is complete. Cash includes short-term investments; debt and minority interests come off.
  • India. The risk-free rate has to be free of default risk, so the rupee government bond yield has the sovereign default spread removed (that risk is already inside the 7.1% premium). For companies earning most of their revenue abroad, such as IT services, the premium is blended toward the mature-market figure.
On this site: the valuation page shows the model used under the method switch, and “Where the starting numbers came from” lists the reason behind every default. When the model and the market disagree by a factor of two, a check tells you to look at the data first, then at what the price assumes.

Banks, insurers and other special cases

A standard cash-flow DCF assumes you can separate how a business operates from how it is financed. That breaks down in a few places, and Damodaran's notes treat each one differently. Fulcrum follows the same split.

Financial firms: value the equity directly

For a bank or lender, debt is not a funding choice, it is the raw material: deposits and borrowings are what it lends out. Capital spending and working capital are not well defined, and "operating income" before interest is meaningless. So the notes value the equity directly. The cash flow to shareholders is what the firm earns, minus what it must keep to support growth (regulators require capital to rise as the loan book grows).

Paid to shareholders = net income − growth in book equity
Value = book equity + present value of (return on equity − cost of equity) × book equity

The second line is the same answer written as excess returns. A bank that earns exactly its cost of equity is worth exactly its book value (1.0× book). One that earns more is worth more than book; one that earns less destroys value as it grows, and is worth less than book.

On this site: when a company looks like a bank, insurer or lender, the Valuation page switches to the equity model. The sliders become growth in book equity (loan-book growth), return on equity, competitive advantage and cost of equity. The Peers page then compares on P/E and P/B and plots P/B against return on equity, which is the relationship that drives bank multiples. You can switch methods yourself at the top of the sliders.

Insurers, payment networks and asset managers

Insurers are valued on book equity and return on equity too, but their book value includes reserves and investment gains that swing from year to year, so treat the answer as a range. Payment networks, exchanges and data firms are classed as financial in many data feeds, yet they borrow little and behave like ordinary asset-light businesses: Fulcrum values those with the cash-flow DCF.

Cyclical and commodity companies

For oil, metals, chemicals and similar businesses, today's margin is often a peak or a trough. The notes recommend normalising: value the firm on an average margin through the cycle rather than the latest year. Fulcrum sets the target margin on the multi-year average for these sectors (a blend of today's and the average for semiconductors, hardware and industrials), and you can override it.

Real estate

Property companies carry large depreciation that does not reflect real wear, so operating income understates cash earnings. Analysts use funds from operations and net asset value instead. Treat the DCF as a cross-check and lean on EV/EBITDA and P/B on the Peers page.

Loss-making and very young companies

If a company loses money today, extrapolating its current margin gives a negative value. Instead you set the margin it could reach when mature and how fast it gets there, and let the reinvestment rules tell you what the growth costs.

Common mistakes this site tries to prevent

Easy to do

  • Letting growth run above the risk-free rate forever
  • Zero reinvestment in the terminal year
  • Using a peer multiple as the exit value
  • Averaging multiples that include negatives and outliers
  • Applying an enterprise multiple and forgetting net debt
  • Valuing a bank like a factory

What Fulcrum does

  • Caps terminal growth at the risk-free rate
  • Sets reinvestment to growth ÷ return on capital
  • No exit multiple; intrinsic value only
  • Medians; drops undefined multiples; shows the sample size
  • Subtracts net debt for EV multiples
  • Warns that FCFF DCF does not suit banks and insurers

Also remember what a DCF cannot do: it will not tell you the right story, and a precise-looking answer is only as good as the assumptions underneath. The “What the price implies” chart is there so you can ask the more honest question: is the story the market is pricing in believable?
